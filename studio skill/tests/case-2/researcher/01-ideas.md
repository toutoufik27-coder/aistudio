## I-1: The Street-Birth Puzzle

Subject / event: Ignaz Semmelweis's investigation of childbed fever at the Vienna General Hospital, 1840s.
Protagonist: Ignaz Semmelweis.
Angle: The investigation, not the downfall. The story is the puzzle (why did women who gave birth in the street do better than women in the doctors' ward?) and it ends when he finds the answer.
Why it is a story:
- Want: to learn why one of two near-identical maternity wards kills far more mothers than the other.
- Obstacle: each explanation he tests fails, and the true cause is the doctors' own hands, his included.
- Non-obvious result: the clue comes from the death of a colleague, not from a patient.

Fact:
- None verified this session.

Dramatization needed:
- His private reactions and the order in which he considered explanations, unless his 1861 book gives them; to be inferred from that text only.
- Any individual patient. No named patient appears in the leads; a composite must be labeled as such or avoided.

Unverified or disputed:
- unverified: First Clinic mortality about 10% against under 4% in the Second Clinic; one summary of his 1861 book gives 9.9% and 3.3% [S]. Figures vary by source.
- unverified: in 1846 the First Clinic rate was five times that of the Second [L1].
- unverified: childbed fever was rare among women who had street births, and this puzzled him [S].
- unverified: his colleague Kolletschka was cut by a student's scalpel during a post-mortem and died of a disease Semmelweis judged identical to the mothers' [S].
- unverified: after chlorinated-lime handwashing, First Clinic annual mortality fell from 11.4% in 1846 to 1.3% in 1848 [L2].
- disputed: the 1846 figure appears as about 10%, 9.9%, and 11.4% across the search summaries; the period each covers must be checked.
- Not found at all: the date handwashing began, the date of Kolletschka's death, and the list of explanations Semmelweis ruled out.

Similarity risks:
- Heavily covered subject. Search returned an Al Jazeera animated episode, "Unsung Hero: Ignaz Semmelweis" (2013) [L3]; a History Guy video, "The Doctor Who Learned to Wash His Hands" (2020) [L4]; a Weird History video on his struggle to convince peers [L5]; a Google Doodle (20 March 2020) [L6].
- From titles and summaries these lean on the rejection story, which is the angle already in the log. Whether any is built around the street-birth puzzle was not checked.

Ratings:
- Originality: low. One of the most retold stories in medical history; only the structure differs.
- Emotional potential: high. The cause turns out to be the investigator's own profession.
- Narrative depth: high. A sequence of failed explanations, then one decisive clue.
- Audience appeal: high. Familiar name, and a mystery structure holds attention over 6 minutes.
- Production feasibility: medium. Two wards and one autopsy room suit flat 2D, but nothing is verified yet and ward scenes imply many figures.

Sources: none opened.

## I-2: Three Million Steps to Lhasa

Subject / event: The secret route survey from Nepal to Lhasa, 1865-66.
Protagonist: Nain Singh, a surveyor trained by the Survey of India.
Angle: A man maps a country closed to outsiders using his own body as the instrument, counting every pace while disguised.
Why it is a story:
- Want: to fix the position of Lhasa and the route to it.
- Obstacle: he must measure without ever being seen measuring.
- Non-obvious result: a walked count of paces produces a usable map.

Fact:
- None verified this session.

Dramatization needed:
- His thoughts on the road and the moments of near discovery, unless Montgomerie's published report describes them.
- The British motive (imperial intelligence) needs honest framing; that is a choice of emphasis, not invention.

Unverified or disputed:
- unverified: he and his cousin Mani Singh left Dehra Dun on 8 January 1865 [L7].
- unverified: the two separated; Nain Singh reached Lhasa on 10 January 1866 [L7].
- unverified: Thomas Montgomerie chose them on the advice of Edmund Smyth [S].
- unverified: he walked 1,580 miles, counted as 3,160,000 paces [S].
- unverified: stride trained to 33 inches [S]; another summary says 2,000 steps to the mile [S]. These two figures do not agree (2,000 paces of 33 inches is 5,500 feet, not 5,280) and must be reconciled.
- unverified: he counted on a 100-bead rosary, one bead per 100 paces [S].
- unverified: he spun a prayer wheel to keep curious travellers away [S].
- unverified: he received the Royal Geographical Society's Patron's Medal [S].
- unverified: the published account is Montgomerie's 1868 "Report of a Route-Survey Made by Pundit, from Nepal to Lhasa" [S].

Similarity risks:
- Search returned articles and museum pages (Atlas Obscura [L10], Whipple Museum [L11]) and a 2017 Google Doodle [L12], but no video on him. This is absence in one web search, not evidence that none exists.

Ratings:
- Originality: medium. Known in India and among map enthusiasts; no video turned up in search.
- Emotional potential: medium. Tension and endurance, but little is known of his inner life.
- Narrative depth: medium. A journey with a clear goal; the instrument tricks carry it.
- Audience appeal: high. A simple hook: he counted every step.
- Production feasibility: medium. One protagonist, mountain landscapes, and small props suit flat 2D well; held at medium because nothing is verified and Lhasa street scenes need period care.

## I-3: The Filth Parties

Subject / event: Joseph Goldberger's pellagra work in the US South, 1914-16.
Protagonist: Joseph Goldberger.
Angle: To prove a disease is not contagious, a doctor tries as hard as he can to catch it.
Why it is a story:
- Want: to show that pellagra comes from diet, not a germ.
- Obstacle: the diet answer points at poverty, and his critics prefer a germ.
- Non-obvious result: his proof is a failure to get sick, and he dies without finding the missing nutrient.

Fact:
- None verified this session.

Dramatization needed:
- Volunteers' reactions during the self-experiments, unless his papers or letters record them.
- The ethics of the prison experiment (pardons in exchange for risk) must be stated plainly, not softened.

Unverified or disputed:
- unverified: a 1915 experiment at the Rankin State Prison Farm, with pardons offered through Governor Earl Brewer [S].
- disputed: 11 volunteers [S] or 12 [S]; five developed pellagra [S] or six showed dermatitis within six months [S].
- unverified: on 26 April 1916 he injected five cubic centimetres of a patient's blood into his assistant George Wheeler [S].
- unverified: he called these sessions "filth parties"; Mary Goldberger took part; material included skin, blood, urine, and feces; no volunteer got pellagra [S]. The phrase is not confirmed as his own wording.
- unverified: he never identified the nutrient; publicly vindicated in 1927 [S].

Similarity risks:

Ratings:

- Emotional potential: medium. Disgust and stubbornness more than sorrow.
- Narrative depth: high. Science, poverty, and regional pride pull against each other.
- Audience appeal: high. The hook is strong and uncomfortable.
- Production feasibility: low. Prison farm, orphanage, and skin-disease imagery are hard to render tastefully in flat 2D within 7 days, and the key numbers conflict.
