## I-1: The Ward That Killed

Subject / event: Ignaz Semmelweis's investigation of childbed fever in the Vienna maternity wards (dates unverified).
Protagonist: Ignaz Semmelweis.
Angle: The investigation, not the downfall. One hospital, one ward staffed by doctors and students, one by midwives, and far more mothers dying in the first. He eliminates one explanation after another until a colleague's death gives him the answer. The story ends at the discovery.

Why it is a story:
- Want: to learn why mothers die in one ward and not in the other.
- Obstacle: every accepted explanation fails against his own ward records, and the true cause is the doctors themselves.
- Non-obvious result: the answer comes from the death of a friend, not from a patient.

Fact:
- None verified this session. See the next section.

Dramatization needed:
- His private reactions to each failed explanation, and to his colleague's death; inferred, because the narration needs a thread between the recorded steps.
- The look of the wards, the dissection room, and the washing basin; period reconstruction.
- Whether mothers pleaded to be admitted to the midwives' ward is often repeated; use it only if a source supports it.

Unverified or disputed:
- unverified: the hospital had a ward for training medical students and a ward for training midwives, and deaths from childbed fever were several times higher in the students' ward.
- unverified: he ruled out overcrowding and climate because both were the same or worse in the safer ward.
- unverified: a colleague, Jakob Kolletschka, died after being cut during an autopsy, with findings like those in the dead mothers.
- unverified: washing hands in a chlorinated lime solution brought the death rate in the students' ward down to about the level of the midwives' ward.
- unverified: the size of the mortality figures. Search summaries give different numbers for different months and years; these must be taken from his own tables or a scholarly article.

Similarity risks:
- The subject is heavily covered. Search listings show a History Guy video, a Weird History video, an Al Jazeera animated episode, a PBS Origin of Everything episode, and an older short film. None was opened.
- From the listings, most appear to tell the life story and the rejection. A version that stays inside the investigation and stops at the answer would differ in structure, but that cannot be confirmed without watching them.

Ratings:
- Originality: low — one of the most retold stories in medical history; only the structure is different.
- Emotional potential: high — mothers dying, and the cause is the people trying to help them.
- Narrative depth: high — a real chain of failed hypotheses with a turning point.
- Audience appeal: high — familiar name, clear stakes, a puzzle to follow.
- Production feasibility: medium — few locations and a small cast suit the method, but the central events are unverified, and hospital wards with many patients are hard to keep consistent in AI images.

Sources: none opened.

## I-2: The Curate Who Tried to Clear the Pump

Subject / event: The Broad Street cholera outbreak in London and the parish inquiry that followed (dates unverified).
Protagonist: Henry Whitehead, a young curate of the local parish.
Angle: The famous story belongs to John Snow and his map. This one follows the man who thought Snow was wrong, went door to door to prove it, and found the evidence that proved Snow right.

Why it is a story:
- Want: to clear the neighbourhood pump of blame, because he knows these families and does not believe the water theory.
- Obstacle: his own inquiry; the more households he traces, the more the deaths follow the pump.
- Non-obvious result: the sceptic finds the first case and the leaking cesspool, and becomes the theory's strongest witness.

Fact:
- None verified this session. See the next section.

Dramatization needed:
- His state of mind as his findings turned against his own belief; inferred from the sequence of his reports.
- The house visits; the households he spoke to are documented only in summary, so individual encounters would be invented and must be labeled.
- Street and interior detail of the neighbourhood.

Unverified or disputed:
- unverified: Whitehead was a curate at the parish church that served Broad Street when the outbreak began.
- unverified: he began his inquiry in order to disprove Snow's theory.
- unverified: he traced a large share of the street's residents through house-to-house inquiry.
- unverified: he identified an infant at a house beside the pump as the first case, and an excavation found the house's cesspool leaking toward the well.
- disputed, per a search summary: the doctor who attended the infant did not accept that the child died of cholera.
- unverified: Whitehead and Snow sat on the same parish inquiry committee and remained on good terms.

Similarity risks:
- John Snow and the pump are very heavily covered. Search listings show a multi-part Extra History series, a Vox video on the map, a SciShow video, and a Royal Institution talk. None was opened.
- No listing was found for a video centred on Whitehead. That is a search result, not proof that none exists. Steven Johnson's book The Ghost Map is known to give Whitehead a large role, so the angle is not new in print.

Ratings:
- Originality: medium — the event is famous; the protagonist and his reversal are much less told.
- Emotional potential: medium — a pastor among dying parishioners is strong, but the turn is intellectual.
- Narrative depth: high — a protagonist who changes his mind on evidence, with a clear before and after.
- Audience appeal: medium — viewers who know the Snow story get a new side of it; others need the background first, which costs runtime.
- Production feasibility: medium — maps and street plans suit flat 2D well, but crowded period streets are costly, and the central events are unverified.

Sources: none opened.

## I-3: The Line Down the Middle of the Atlantic

Subject / event: The discovery of the rift valley along the Mid-Atlantic Ridge (dates unverified).
Protagonist: Marie Tharp, a geologist and draftswoman at a university ocean laboratory.
Angle: She was not allowed on the research ships, so she drew the sea floor from other people's soundings, and saw on paper a valley that supported an idea her field treated as close to heresy. Her colleague dismissed it. The story is how a drawing won the argument.

Why it is a story:
- Want: to draw the ocean floor accurately from the sounding data.
- Obstacle: what she draws implies the continents move, and her own colleague will not accept it.
- Non-obvious result: an unrelated plot of earthquake locations lands exactly along her valley, and the doubter becomes her co-author.

Fact:
- None verified this session. See the next section.

Dramatization needed:
- The working hours at the drafting table; routine detail inferred from how such profiles were made.
- Her reaction to the dismissal; only her later recollection is likely to exist, and it should be presented as her account.
- The moment the two maps are compared; the event is reported, the staging would be invented.

Unverified or disputed:
- unverified: women were not permitted on the laboratory's research ships at the time.
- unverified: she noticed a V-shaped notch at the crest of the ridge in several profiles across the North Atlantic.
- unverified: Bruce Heezen first dismissed the finding in words she later quoted, and had her redo the work.
- unverified: a plot of earthquake epicentres made for another project lined up with the valley, and this changed his mind.
- disputed, per a search summary: the year of the discovery differs between her account and his, and the sharing of credit is contested.

Similarity risks:
- Search listings show an animated Royal Institution short narrated by Helen Czerski, a SciShow "Great Minds" episode, a National Geographic short, and a Britannica video. None was opened.
- The Royal Institution film is animated and biographical, which is close in form. A version confined to the one argument over the rift, without the full biography, would be narrower. This cannot be confirmed without watching it.

Ratings:
- Originality: medium — several short videos exist; most appear to be whole-life summaries.
- Emotional potential: medium — dismissal and vindication, without physical danger.
- Narrative depth: medium — one clean reversal; the science needs careful explanation in little time.
- Audience appeal: medium — the maps are striking, the name is less known.
- Production feasibility: medium — one room, two people, and maps suit flat 2D very well; held at medium because the central events are unverified.

Sources: none opened.

## I-4: The Seamstress on Wrangel Island

Subject / event: The Wrangel Island expedition and its only survivor (dates unverified).
Protagonist: Ada Blackjack, an Iñupiat woman hired to sew for the party.
Angle: She was the one member with no exploring ambition and no Arctic survival training. When the men left or died, she taught herself to trap, shoot, and keep a camp, and was the only one found alive.

Why it is a story:
- Want: wages to care for her sick son; then simply to live long enough to see him.
- Obstacle: the relief ship does not come, the food runs out, the able men leave across the ice, and she is left nursing a dying man and afraid of the bears.
- Non-obvious result: the least-regarded member of the expedition is the one who survives it.

Fact:
- None verified this session. See the next section.

Dramatization needed:
- Day-to-day actions between her diary entries; inferred from the entries and from the other diary.
- Her thoughts, beyond what her diary records.
- The appearance of the camp and the island across seasons.

Unverified or disputed:
- unverified: the expedition was organised by Vilhjalmur Stefansson to support a territorial claim to the island.
- unverified: the party was a small group of young men, plus Blackjack, hired in Nome to sew and cook.
- unverified: she took the work to pay for treatment for her son.
- unverified: after a relief ship failed to reach the island, most of the men set out over the ice for help and were not seen again.
- unverified: the remaining man, Lorne Knight, was ill with scurvy and died while she cared for him.
- unverified: she kept a diary in the last months, and it and Knight's diary survive in a university library.
- disputed, per a search summary: parts of Knight's diary are crossed out or damaged, and who did it is contested; the start date of that diary differs between descriptions.
- unverified: how she was treated by the men, and by the organiser and the press afterward.

Similarity risks:
- Search listings show a short documentary film, "Ada Blackjack Rising", a podcast-style video episode, and many articles. A well-known book by Jennifer Niven covers her life. None was opened.
- The existing short film is reported to be about the same length as this project's format. The difference here would be the animated form and a strict use of the two diaries as the spine. This cannot be confirmed without watching it.

Ratings:
- Originality: medium — known in print and in one short film; rare in animated explainers, from what the listings show.
- Emotional potential: high — a mother alone, a dying companion, and a long wait.
- Narrative depth: high — she changes from dependent to self-reliant, and the aftermath is morally mixed.
- Audience appeal: high — survival stories with a single protagonist hold attention.
- Production feasibility: medium — one main character, one location, and snow and sky suit a limited palette; held at medium because the central events are unverified and because depicting an Iñupiat woman and her clothing accurately needs reference images, not guesses.

Sources: none opened.

## I-5: Read by a Man

Subject / event: An early experiment on how different gases warm in sunlight, and its presentation at a national science meeting (dates unverified).
Protagonist: Eunice Foote, an amateur experimenter.
Angle: With glass cylinders and thermometers she found that one gas warmed far more than air, and wrote that an atmosphere of it would warm the earth. A man read her paper to the meeting. Then it dropped out of the record for a very long time (length unverified).

Why it is a story:
- Want: to have a finding heard by the scientific community.
- Obstacle: she is an amateur and a woman; she does not read her own paper, and it is left out of the meeting's main record.
- Non-obvious result: a later researcher gets the credit, and her paper is found again only recently, by a geologist reading old annuals (unverified).

Fact:
- None verified this session. See the next section.

Dramatization needed:
- Almost everything about her as a person. Very little about her life and no account of her feelings appear to survive, so a character-led telling would lean heavily on invention.
- The experiment itself can be reconstructed from her paper once it is opened.

Unverified or disputed:
- unverified: she compared the warming of several gases in sunlight and found the greatest warming in carbonic acid gas, the name then used for carbon dioxide.
- unverified: Joseph Henry of the Smithsonian read the paper at the meeting.
- disputed, per search summaries: why she did not read it herself; one summary calls the reason unclear, another points to the standing of amateurs and women.
- unverified: John Tyndall published related work a few years later and is usually credited.
- unverified: whether Tyndall knew of her paper.
- unverified: she signed the Seneca Falls declaration.
- caution: her set-up is generally said not to have isolated the mechanism later measured by Tyndall, so "discovered the greenhouse effect" would overstate it. This needs a source before any wording is chosen.

Similarity risks:
- Search listings show a Science Museum of Virginia video of similar length, a short film titled "Eunice", Crash Course episodes that mention her, and a Yale Climate Connections video. None was opened.
- The subject has had a wave of coverage since her rediscovery. The rediscovery itself as the frame is less common in the listings.

Ratings:
- Originality: low — widely covered in recent years, with one video near this length.
- Emotional potential: medium — the injustice is clear, but there is no recorded personal struggle to show.
- Narrative depth: low — one experiment and one meeting; thin record of the protagonist.
- Audience appeal: high — ties to a present-day subject people search for.
- Production feasibility: medium — glass cylinders, one figure, one hall are simple to draw; held down by the unverified central events and by how much would have to be invented.

Sources: none opened.

## I-6: "Almost Certainly Not Real"

Subject / event: A doctoral thesis on the composition of stellar atmospheres and the objection to its main result (dates unverified).
Protagonist: Cecilia Payne, a doctoral student at the Harvard observatory.
Angle: She calculated that the stars are overwhelmingly hydrogen. The leading astronomer of the day told her that was impossible. She kept the numbers and added a sentence disowning them. A few years later he reached the same result himself.

Why it is a story:
- Want: to finish a doctorate and have her result accepted.
- Obstacle: the result contradicts what every senior astronomer believes, and the one who objects can end her career.
- Non-obvious result: she prints the truth and denies it in the same thesis, and the man who told her it was impossible later confirms it.

Fact:
- None verified this session. See the next section.

Dramatization needed:
- Her decision to add the disclaimer; her later autobiography may describe it, and that should be used in place of invention if it does.
- Visualising spectra and abundance, which is abstraction, not invention, but must not misstate the physics.

Unverified or disputed:
- unverified: she moved from England to Harvard because her English university did not grant degrees to women.
- unverified: Henry Norris Russell wrote to her that the hydrogen abundance was impossible.
- unverified: the thesis text calls the result improbably high and almost certainly not real (the title phrase is quoted from a search summary, not from the thesis).
- unverified: Russell published the same conclusion some years later, cited her, and was credited in textbooks.
- disputed, per a search summary: whether Russell's objection came from prejudice or from reasonable caution about a radical result; his biographer is said to favour caution.

Similarity risks:
- Search listings show a PBS Space Time episode, a Big Think short, and many articles from the centenary of the thesis. None was opened.
- Coverage is recent and plentiful. A version built on the single contradictory sentence in the thesis is narrower than a biography, but the hook is already used as a headline by at least one article in the listings.

Ratings:
- Originality: low — heavy recent coverage and a well-used hook.
- Emotional potential: medium — self-denial under pressure is quiet but strong.
- Narrative depth: medium — the antagonist is not a villain, which is interesting; the action is letters and pages.
- Audience appeal: medium — astronomy draws viewers; the conflict is subtle.
- Production feasibility: medium — few characters and interiors only, but spectra and abundances are hard to show truthfully in flat images, and the central events are unverified.

## Recommendation
